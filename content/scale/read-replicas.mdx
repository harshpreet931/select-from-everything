<InShort>
a **read replica** is a standby that replays the primary's WAL and answers read-only queries. it's always a little behind (**replication lag**), so a read sent there right after a write can miss that write. **read-your-writes** means a user always sees their own changes: route their reads to the primary for a while, or remember the LSN of their last write and wait until the replica has replayed that far.
</InShort>

## the picture

you know how you save your profile, the page reloads, and your old name is still there, and then it fixes itself a second later? that's a read replica answering before it heard about your write.

think of a newsroom (the primary) and a printed copy pinned on a wall down the hall (the standby). anyone can read the wall, which takes load off the newsroom. but the wall is updated by a runner, so for a moment after every story changes, the wall shows the old one.

here it is between two real postgres 18.6 servers. the standby was started with `recovery_min_apply_delay = 500ms`, so the moment is long enough to catch every time:

the standby isn't broken. it's doing exactly what [streaming replication](/learn/wal/streaming-replication) does: WAL arrives, gets written, then gets replayed into pages, and a query on the standby sees whatever has been replayed. the LSNs say precisely how far behind it is.

## how much lag

without the artificial delay, on one machine with nothing else running, the standby replays in well under a millisecond. but a read sent right after the commit still usually loses the race:

<Explain>
maybe the old value. with asynchronous replication, COMMIT returns once the primary has flushed its own WAL; the standby hasn't necessarily received it, let alone replayed it. even `synchronous_commit = on` with a synchronous standby only waits for the standby to flush, not replay. only `remote_apply` makes COMMIT wait until the standby has replayed it, at the price of every commit waiting for the slowest synchronous standby.
</Explain>

## routing reads

a replica only takes load off the primary if reads go to it. something has to decide which reads: a driver setting, the ORM's database router, or a proxy in front. the policy is the interesting part:

<Figure wide caption={<>one user saving and reloading. pick a routing policy and drag the lag. <C k="dead">stale</C> reads are the ones that miss the user's own last save.</>}>
<ReplicaRouting />
</Figure>

- **everything to the replica.** fine for reads that can be a little old (dashboards, search, other people's posts). wrong for "show me what I just did".
- **stick to the primary after a write.** route a user's reads to the primary for a few seconds after they write. simple, and only as right as your guess about lag.
- **wait for the LSN.** after a write, the app reads `pg_current_wal_lsn()` (or `pg_current_wal_insert_lsn()`) on the primary and keeps it with the user's session. before reading from a replica, it checks the replica's `pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()` and waits, or falls back to the primary, until that has passed the saved LSN. exact, and the cost is a short wait right after a write.

both functions run anywhere, and the replay one has a quirk worth knowing. it reports where the last WAL replay ended, standby or not. a primary that was shut down cleanly has never replayed anything and says NULL. the browser's postgres did replay: it starts from a snapshot of a data directory and runs crash recovery on it, so it reports where that ended (a native primary does the same after a crash and restart; checked on a throwaway 18.6):

```sql run
SELECT pg_is_in_recovery() AS is_standby, pg_last_wal_replay_lsn() AS replayed;
```

so check `pg_is_in_recovery()` first, and only trust the replay position on a server that says true.

and LSNs compare and subtract like numbers, which is all the wait loop needs:

```sql run
SELECT '0/302FCB0'::pg_lsn >= '0/302FC88'::pg_lsn AS caught_up,
       pg_wal_lsn_diff('0/302FCB0', '0/302FC88') AS bytes_behind;
```

those two LSNs are the recorded ones: the write at `0/302FCB0`, and the standby's replay position when it answered "ada".

postgres 19 <Since v={19} preview /> adds a command for the waiting part. run on a standby, it blocks until replay reaches the LSN (or a timeout passes) and returns a status:

```text
WAIT FOR LSN '0/302FCB0' WITH (TIMEOUT '1s', NO_THROW);
```

it has to be a top-level statement, outside a transaction that holds a snapshot or locks, because a waiting session that holds them could block the very replay it's waiting for ([the 19 docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/19/sql-wait.html)).

<Gotcha title="a replica is also a source of conflicts">
a long query on a hot standby needs old row versions that the primary may already have vacuumed away. replay then either waits (and lag grows) or cancels the query with a recovery conflict, depending on `max_standby_streaming_delay`. `hot_standby_feedback = on` makes the primary keep those versions, which moves the bloat to the primary instead. [streaming replication](/learn/wal/streaming-replication) has the details; for whole-row change streams to other systems, see [logical replication](/learn/wal/logical-replication).
</Gotcha>

## what replicas don't do

replicas scale reads, not writes: every write still goes through the one primary, and every replica replays every write. they don't make a single slow query faster. and a failover promotes a replica that may be missing the last commits unless replication was synchronous. when the write volume itself outgrows one machine, the next step is [sharding](/learn/scale/sharding).

<Interview q="how do you guarantee read-your-writes with read replicas?">
after a user writes, either send that user's reads to the primary for a while, or record the commit's LSN (`pg_current_wal_lsn()` on the primary) in their session and only read from a replica whose `pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()` has passed it, waiting or falling back to the primary otherwise. the LSN approach is exact; the time window is a guess about lag. bonus point: `synchronous_commit = remote_apply` makes every commit wait for the synchronous standbys to replay, which gives read-your-writes on those standbys for everyone, at a cost on every commit.
</Interview>

<Keylines>
- a replica replays the primary's WAL, so it's always a little behind.
- a read right after a commit can lose that race, even on one machine.
- route reads that can be old to replicas; route "my own data" carefully.
- read-your-writes: stick to the primary after a write, or wait for the write's LSN.
- replicas scale reads, never writes.
</Keylines>
